Ideology, Institutions, and Wisdom

24 October 2026

APPLICABLE TAGS:

The point of philosophy and the study of ideology is to determine the answers to four questions: where did we come from, where are we going, who are we, and how do we relate to each other? Philosophy, specifically, adds on more fundamental questions, but most important of all, it asks what is the truth? Philosophy, after all, translates to “the love of wisdom”. As St. Augustine relates of his reading of Cicero, “the only thing that pleased me … was his advice not simply to admire one or another of the schools of philosophy, but to love wisdom itself, whatever it might be, and to search for it, pursue it, hold it, and embrace it firmly” (Confessions). Indeed, people who study philosophy and ideology both ultimately seek the truth. But, because our world is largely imperfect, flawed, and complicated, people have needed to construct large systems of ideas to explain it, and when ideas become integrated into such large systems, they seem to become alive, existent, independent in a profound sense. It is as if the unity that they consist in gives them their own kind of life.

This is why we observe what was expounded by Terry Eagleton: “One can understand well enough how human beings may struggle and murder for good material reasons … It is much harder to grasp how they may come to do so in the name of something as apparently abstract as ideas” (Ideology: An Introduction). It manifests because beliefs and values “crystallize” into a whole that is more alive than it would be if these values remained separate and underdeveloped.

When beliefs and values crystallize into this new kind of life, something in the pursuit of wisdom seems to become fragile. To be precise, an incentive appears, an incentive to defend the life of one’s ideas. It is one difference between common, everyday racism and the racism of the Nazis: the former is a prejudice, the latter is an ideology. The former may be something that one can be convinced out of, if found out early enough; the latter is something which people may die in the defense of.

Furthermore, ideologies, as we know, often coalesce into movements, institutions, and bureaucracies, which is how the life of an ideology transitions from the abstract plane to the concrete world (as much as it can, anyway - ultimately, an institution is still a social construct). The U.S. republic, for example, is founded on one belief - namely, the belief in “Life, Liberty, and the Pursuit of Happiness”; but, in practice, it is founded on a lot more than that. It is founded on an ideology that is indeed based on the beliefs and values expressed through that slogan, but has incurred numerous accretions and philosophical developments from the likes of John Locke, Charles de Secondat, Thomas Hobbes, Jean-Jacque Rousseau, Thomas Paine, etc.

As a counterweight, we may consider the USSR and how it was founded on the communist principle of “from each according to ability, to each according to need”, but in practice has its own complicated ideology based on contributions from such figures as Karl Marx, Friedrich Engels, Leon Trotsky, Joseph Stalin, etc.

We also know how, in the process of coalescing, ideologies and the institutions they inspire may seem to become entirely unreceptive to change, or contradictory. For instance, in the Cold War, the USA was unwavering in its ideological commitments to the extent that it adopted downright illiberal foreign policies in order to further its own ideological vision: a rather scandalous contradiction. It would also seem illogical that the USA, if it were truly ideologically honest and consistent, would approve of such policies as propping up bloody dictators in Latin America. But that is the point: the USA is not completely ideologically honest and consistent, and neither was the USSR. Not a lot of institutions at this level of size and influence can maintain the logical consistency and honesty that the pursuit of wisdom and truth demands, because, once again, the incentive to prioritize their own continued existence takes precedence.

It is not a critique of the USA or the USSR specifically, since all institutions, religions, and movements suffer from this incentive. It is a vulnerability of the standard institution and ideology that its original purpose, however noble, is corrupted by the organic desire to continue existing. This is why it is far easier for one individual to challenge their own ideas, beliefs, and values than it is for an institutionalized ideology to be challenged. Therefore, institutions and ideologies may become barren wastelands for people wanting to really actualize the purpose of philosophy.

No wonder many people become disillusioned at ideologies and institutions. But it is crucial to realize that all ideology and all worldly philosophy is founded on the very same pursuit of truth that they may now obscure. Neither is the adverse incentive I outlined something which totally applies to or disqualifies ideologies in general, indiscriminately. Us humans likely cannot help from building idea-systems that try to explain the world, anyway. The point is that it is worth it to remember that true wisdom starts in a more fundamental place than these. As St. Paul says:“Beware lest any man spoil you through philosophy and vain deceit, following the tradition of men according to the rudiments of the world, and not in accordance with Christ” (Colossians 2:8). It is a good exercise to think about what he means here, even if you’re not Christian, because he correctly notes the human tendency to become overly attached to our elaborate systems of logic while becoming out of touch with what value or what need legitimizes those systems of logic in the first place. For philosophers in the Christian tradition, that would be Christ. For you, it might be different. For everyone, Christian or not, what legitimizes any philosophy is how well it helps one pursue the truth and embrace it. It’s something to think about.
